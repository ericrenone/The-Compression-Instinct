## Part I: Three in the Morning in Nagoya

### The flyer job

He gets home at two or three in the morning. The job is flyers. Almost all flyers, with the occasional poster, for a design firm in Nagoya. He is in his early twenties. He went to a high school with a design course because he wanted to be a graphic designer, and this is what graphic design turned out to be: lettering, which he is not good at and does not like, and a client who looks at the chicken he has drawn for a poster and says the chicken is the wrong sex.

> "This is a rooster, so it's no good. We only sell hens."
> — a client, as recalled by Akira Toriyama (Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No 2013)

He redraws the chicken.

Because he gets home at three, he cannot get up. He is late. The firm has a rule: three late arrivals count as a full day's absence. He is late a great deal. He is also told off for how he dresses. In his third year the pay is still low, lower than a third-year's pay should be, and one day he does the arithmetic and concludes that this will not do. He quits; no account has him fired. He is twenty-three. He has no job. He asks his mother for money.

He will say later that he hated it. He will say, in the same breath, that it might have come in handy. He will say, more than once and for the rest of his life, that he never wanted to draw comics at all.

### What a flyer teaches

A flyer has no reader. It has a passer-by. Everything on it has to work for a person who did not ask to see it, at a distance, in a glance, with no goodwill and no second look. The firm did not teach the young man to draw; he could already draw. It taught him what an uneven line costs, and what happens to a page when a stranger has to read it from across a street. He never stopped hating uneven lines. "I hate it when the linework is uneven, or the art is poor," he said four decades later, as if the client were still standing behind him (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews).

The firm also taught him to letter by hand. He said he was forced into it. He said he was bad at it. In 1977, unemployed, he sent a parody of *Star Wars* to the newcomer contest of a weekly manga magazine. It was disqualified; parodies were not eligible. An editor read it anyway, found it "beautiful and funny," and sent a telegram telling the author to keep drawing and to send the work directly to him (Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima; Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No). Years afterward the editor explained why he had bothered with a disqualified entry from a man who had never drawn a comic. He did not mention the story. He said: "You were good at hand-lettering."

That is the hinge. A design firm he hated taught him the one skill that a stranger would notice first. The stranger noticed. The young man was Akira Toriyama, born April 5, 1955, in Kiyosu, outside Nagoya, who would draw *Dr. Slump* and *Dragon Ball* and every monster in *Dragon Quest* from a home studio in the same town, and who would die in 2024 as one of the most widely imitated draftsmen alive.

Forty days before him, on February 24, 1955, a boy had been born in San Francisco and placed for adoption with a machinist and a bookkeeper. Seventeen years later that boy would drop out of college after one semester, stay on campus for a year and a half anyway, and wander into a calligraphy class taught by a former Trappist monk. He would say later that it had no hope of any practical application in his life. He would say it all came back ten years on.

Neither of them trained for the thing he became. Both of them were taught, by a discipline next door, what a stranger notices first.

## Part II: The Story We Tell Is Wrong

### Taste and laziness

We tell ourselves a story about where good design comes from. The story is wrong.

The story has two versions, and they sound like opposites. The first is the taste story: Steve Jobs had an eye. He saw Kyoto gardens and Braun radios and Zen rock and a calligrapher's italic hand, and the seeing poured out of him as the Macintosh and the iPod and the iPhone. Simplicity was his gift, and the rest of us can only admire it. The second is the laziness story: Akira Toriyama was a slacker. He made the Super Saiyan's hair blond so he would not have to ink it. He blew up the tournament ring because it was a pain to draw. He gave his characters flight so he could skip the vehicles. *Dragon Ball* is what happens when a lazy genius cuts corners, and the corners happened to be beautiful.

Both stories are told by the men themselves. Jobs moralized his simplicity; he said you have to work hard to get your thinking clean. Toriyama apologized for his; he called himself "a master of the plausible excuse" (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews). One claimed the credit, one disclaimed it. And both were describing the same procedure, which is neither a gift nor a vice. It is a procedure that can be named, broken into parts, and run on purpose.

Call it the compression instinct. Here is the whole of it: take an object and remove marks until it stops being that object. Then put one mark back. Do this under a constraint harsh enough that you cannot cheat, and judged by someone you cannot argue with. What survives is not a simplified version of the thing. It is the thing's identity, isolated, the way a chemist isolates a compound, with everything that was not the identity boiled off.

### A channel with a narrow pipe

Think of it as a bandwidth problem, because that is what it physically was. A 1984 Macintosh had 128 kilobytes of memory and a one-bit screen. A 1999 Japanese phone had, in the words of the man who drew its icons, "really poor" specs, and its emails were capped at 250 characters. A weekly manga magazine wanted fourteen pages every seven days from a man working alone with one assistant. Each of these is a pipe. Each pipe carries only so many bits per second. A designer facing a narrow pipe has two choices. He can send less of the thing, which is lossy and bad. Or he can find out which bits *are* the thing and send only those, which is compression, and which is the entire art.

The surprise is what compression does to a shape's lifespan. A mark built to survive a narrow pipe survives every wider pipe that comes afterward, because it never depended on bandwidth it did not have. A mark built for a wide pipe decays the moment the pipe changes. This is why a two-dot blob from a 1986 Famicom cartridge is still the unchanged mascot of a game series forty years later, while a set of glossy illustrations drawn for a 2008 phone had to be rerendered eight years on. Shapes have a decay rate, and the decay rate is set at birth, by the pipe.

That is the thesis. What follows is the evidence, and the evidence has a spine: two lives that ran in parallel for fifty-six years without touching, and one small object, a smiling pile of excrement eighteen bytes wide, where they finally met.

One showman, one hermit. One built institutions; the other, by his own account, did "all the work start-to-finish by myself" (Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No). They never met. And the objects they left behind have the same four properties: a closed contour, a minimal face, indifference to scale, and the ability to survive being copied badly. The parallel is not mystical. It is what the procedure produces whenever it is run.

## Part III: The Borrowed Ruler (1955–1978)

### A monk at the front of the room

Portland, Oregon, 1973. A part-time instructor stands in front of about eighty students a year, drawn from every department in the college, and teaches them to write with a brush, a pen, a quill, and ink. He is in his early forties. He spent his twenties in a Cistercian monastery in New Mexico and then in Oregon, where he was scribe, choirmaster, and bookbinder, and where his ten-year course toward ordination had included Gregorian chant and letterforms. He left the order in 1968, after the Second Vatican Council changed monastic life. He has never used a computer. He never will. His advice to students is a single sentence: "Whenever you write, write something worth reading" (Reed Magazine, Palladino obituary 2016).

His name is Robert Palladino. In most retellings of the story that follows, his name is replaced by that of his teacher, Lloyd Reynolds, who taught at Reed College from 1929 to 1969 and built the calligraphy program from nothing. Reed's own website still credits the class to Reynolds. Reed's own magazine, in its obituaries of both men, says the student in question studied with Palladino, and the Smithsonian had to run a correction in October 2011 to sort out which man taught what and when (Reed College; Reed Magazine 2011, 2016; Smithsonian Magazine). The chronology settles it. Reynolds retired three years before the student arrived. The man at the front of the room was Palladino, teaching Reynolds's method. Keep both names, because the method is the point.

Reynolds had been hired to teach English and creative writing. In 1934 he found Edward Johnston's *Writing & Illuminating, & Lettering* and called it "a bolt of lightening." He chose the Italic hand of fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Italy for one specific reason: it resembles printed type. His credit course, first offered in 1948–49, was called "alphabetic communication," and it alternated lectures on the history of the alphabet with studio work in the edged pen and in printing types (Reed College, "The Calligraphy Heritage at Reed"). It was a course about typography disguised as a course about handwriting. And it carried a doctrine that reads, today, like a manifesto for anyone drawing an icon:

> "A letter is a letter, not a picture of anything. It is not a picture of rhythmic movement; it *is* rhythmic movement."
> — Lloyd J. Reynolds (Reed Magazine, "Rediscovering Lloyd Reynolds," 2012)

A letter is not a picture of something else. It is a functional object judged by how it moves the eye. Hold that sentence. It will come back as a 12-pixel glyph in a Tokyo office and as two dots on a blue teardrop in Nagoya.

### The dropout in the back

The student had arrived in fall 1972, aged seventeen, from Palo Alto, officially an English major. He was officially a student for one semester. Then he stayed for about eighteen more months, sleeping in Westport Hall, supported by a dean he later called "one of the heroes of my life," repairing equipment for a psychology professor, and auditing whatever he wanted: Shakespeare, modern dance, calligraphy (Reed Magazine, Jobs obituary 2011). He told the story once, definitively, to a crowd of graduates in June 2005:

> "Reed College at that time offered perhaps the best calligraphy instruction in the country. … Because I had dropped out and didn't have to take normal classes, I decided to take a calligraphy class to learn how to do this."
> — Steve Jobs, Stanford commencement, June 12, 2005 (Stanford News)

Look at what he said he learned. Not "beauty." Not "taste." Three things: the difference between serif and sans-serif typefaces; the varying of space between different letter combinations; and "what makes great typography great." He called it "beautiful, historical, artistically subtle in a way that science can't capture." He said it had no hope of practical application. He said that ten years later, designing the first Macintosh, "it all came back," and that without that one class the Mac "would have never had multiple typefaces or proportionally spaced fonts" (Stanford News; Smithsonian Magazine).

Serif versus sans. Letter-pair spacing. A standard of quality. Those are not the contents of a product. They are a ruler. Calligraphy gave Jobs nothing he could ship. It gave him an instrument for measuring whether a thing on a screen was right, and the instrument was five hundred years old, which meant that no one in the personal-computer business in 1983 owned one. Reed's faculty, as it happens, voted calligraphy out of the curriculum in 1984, the year the Macintosh shipped with its beautiful typography, and the course has never returned for credit (Reed College, "The Calligraphy Heritage at Reed"). The ruler left the college and went into the machine.

### The lettering that got noticed

Now go back to Nagoya. Toriyama's ruler was the flyer. He did not choose it any more than Jobs chose Palladino; he fell into a design-course high school because, he said, it was "a no-brainer," and he fell into a design firm because that is what the high school was for (Wikipedia: Akira Toriyama). In his earliest long interview, in 1980, he was still defensive about it. "I came from a design course in high school." "I didn't like design all that much." "I was forced to do things I'm not good with, like lettering." And then the giveaway: "It was right after I had worked as a designer, so the art was really stiff" (Kanzenshuu, Starlog 1980).

Stiff is a designer's word. It means the line is controlled. It means the shapes close. The editor who read the disqualified parody in 1977 was not reading a storyteller; by his own later account the lettering was "really good" and the design sense "refined," and Toriyama agreed in the same conversation: "I'd gotten my start at an advertising agency, so I could at least do that much" (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews). A manga editor sees more pages in a week than he can remember. The thing he notices first is the thing a passer-by notices first on a flyer: whether the letters are clean. The flyer job had trained exactly the reflex a stranger uses to decide whether to keep looking.

There was a second constraint, financial, and it set the style for good. Early on Toriyama had no money for screentone, the adhesive dot-patterns manga artists use for grey, so he learned to work in pure black and white with solid black areas (Wikipedia: Akira Toriyama). That is the idiom of the poster, not the illustrated page. Thirty-five years later Nobuhiko Horie, who ran *Weekly Shōnen Jump*, wrote the verdict for an exhibition catalogue: "His illustration-like drawings, which once seemed disadvantageous, have proved to be just the opposite," because a clean, closed, illustration-finish drawing transfers to animation cels, game sprites, and merchandise without loss (Kanzenshuu, Toriyama Exhibition Essays). What looked like a handicap in a manga magazine was a ruler borrowed from a trade that lives or dies on legibility at a distance.

### The mechanism

Here is the first law of the compression instinct, and it is counterintuitive enough to state plainly. The adjacent discipline does not supply the content. It supplies the *standard*. And it supplies a standard that insiders in the destination field have stopped being able to see.

A computer engineer in 1983 could see whether text was legible. He could not see whether the space between an "A" and a "V" was wrong, because nothing in his training had ever made that visible. Jobs could, because a monk had made him look at it for a year and a half. A manga editor could see whether a story worked. He could not necessarily articulate why one artist's pages read faster than another's. Toriyama's pages read faster because a client who only sold hens had taught him that a stranger will not forgive an unclear mark.

Jobs named the mechanism himself. "Creativity is just connecting things," he told *Wired* in 1996 (Matter VC, reproducing the attribution). "You can't connect the dots looking forward," he told Stanford in 2005. Toriyama never articulated it; he only conceded that the hated job "might have come in handy." But the pattern repeats in every other borrowed ruler in both lives. Jobs took his industrial-design benchmark from Braun and the Bauhaus, which he encountered at the Aspen design conference in June 1981, and told the same conference in 1983 that computers risked becoming "one more piece-of-junk-object" and that the goal was to package them cleanly, "just like Braun does with its electronics." He took his graphic-identity standard from Paul Rand. He took his spiritual discipline from a Sōtō Zen priest named Kōbun Chino Otogawa, who had begun teaching at a small zendo in Los Altos in the late summer of 1970, who shared calligraphy with him, and who ran the strictest standard of all (Smithsonian/Isaacson; Steve Jobs Archive; Tricycle; Wikipedia: Kōbun Chino Otogawa).

The Kōbun story is the borrowed ruler in its purest form. After India, barefoot and broke, Jobs told his teacher, "I feel I'm enlightened." Kōbun replied that enlightened persons have to show their enlightenment with a concrete example, with proof. A week later Jobs came back with the first Apple computer (Tricycle). Whether that is reportage or parable hardly matters. It is the standard that matters: no claim counts until an object exists. A Zen priest, of all people, supplied Silicon Valley with its demo culture.

Toriyama's other rulers were film and plastic. He saw Disney's *One Hundred and One Dalmatians* in 1961 and said it made him want to draw; he "practically devoured" the art design of Disney animation (Wikipedia; Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No). He built military plastic models and admired weapon props because "Nothing is wasted in the designs" (Kanzenshuu, Starlog 1980). He would watch Jackie Chan films while inking. Disney gave him the closed contour. Model kits gave him a mechanical conscience. Hong Kong cinema gave him pacing. None of these were manga, and manga is what he made with them.

### Two boys, one ocean

Pause on the direction of travel. The American boy's rulers came, overwhelmingly, from Japan and from Europe: a Zen teacher from Niigata, a Walkman handed to him by Akio Morita, the gardens of Kyoto, which he called "the most sublime thing I've ever seen," Braun, the Bauhaus, an Italian hand taught by a monk. The Japanese boy's rulers came from America and Hong Kong: Disney, *Star Wars* (which he parodied for his contest entry), *Alien* (his favorite film), Jackie Chan's *Drunken Master* (without which, he said in 2003, "I would never have come up with Dragon Ball"), Frank Frazetta's illustration collections, all of which he owned, and German and American tank kits (Kanzenshuu, Shonen Jump 2003; Starlog 1980; Nippon.com; Smithsonian/Isaacson). Each man's "native" aesthetic was partly the other's culture, refined and sent back. The compression instinct does not belong to Japan, however often it is attributed to Zen, and it does not belong to California, however often it is attributed to garages. It belongs to anyone who imports a ruler from next door and refuses to put it down.

By 1978 both men had their rulers and nothing else. Jobs had founded Apple on April 1, 1976, at twenty, in his parents' house on Crist Drive; the first brochure for the Apple II, in 1977, carried the headline "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication," and his investor Mike Markkula had written a marketing philosophy in three words that would govern the next thirty years (Wikipedia: Steve Jobs; Smithsonian/Isaacson; Steve Jobs Archive). Toriyama had made his debut in *Weekly Shōnen Jump* in 1978 with a story called *Wonder Island*. It finished last in the reader survey. The sequel flopped too (Wikipedia: Akira Toriyama). The ruler was in hand. What neither man had yet was someone to hit him with it.

## Part IV: The Numbered No (1977–1984)

### Five hundred pages

The telegram had said to send the work directly to him. So Toriyama did, and the editor rejected it. He sent more. The editor rejected that. By Toriyama's own count, given in 1986, about five hundred pages went back before his debut. By the editor's count, given thirty years later, it was about five hundred pages over the eighteen months before *Dr. Slump*. The two men agree on the number and disagree on the window, which is a good reason to treat the figure as approximate and a better reason to notice that both of them remembered it as a number at all (Kanzenshuu, TeLePAL 1986; Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima).

Why did he keep sending? Not vocation. "I was confident in my art, so the fact that I lost out was really frustrating," he said. "Almost entirely out of stubbornness, I set my heart on not giving up until I got the prize money" (Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No). And what did the rejections teach? He answered that too, and the answer is a complete theory of learning in two sentences: "I had no idea about how to draw a comic, so every single one of them was rejected. So it was like, as I drew, I gradually learned how to do it."

The editor was Kazuhiko Torishima, and he was not rejecting pages out of cruelty or caprice. He was running a system. In his later accounts of the job, an editor at *Jump* is three people: a director, who judges storyboards; a manager; and a producer, who develops an artist over three to five years. Torishima limited himself to about five artists. He kept meetings to about thirty minutes. He read every storyboard twice (Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima). And behind him stood the instrument that made the whole thing non-negotiable: the weekly reader survey, in which the children who bought the magazine ranked every chapter in it. *Wonder Island* had finished last. That is not an opinion. That is a number, and the artist cannot argue with a number.

### The second law

Here is the second law of the compression instinct. A ruler is not enough. Someone has to hit you with it, and the someone must be external, and the hit must come with a quantity attached. Call it the numbered no.

A plain "no" can be argued with. Artists argue with editors every day and win, and the work gets worse. A numbered "no" cannot be argued with, because it is not the editor's opinion; it is the editor reading an instrument. Torishima said as much to his own staff: when readers respond strongly, the editor-in-chief's view is "basically irrelevant" (Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima). The survey held the veto. The editor was its interpreter. The artist's job was to satisfy it in as few strokes as possible, because he had fourteen pages to deliver every seven days and one assistant to help him.

But Torishima also understood the instrument's limit, and this is where his doctrine becomes more than A/B testing. A survey, he said, reflects only one moment. The editor's job is to form a hypothesis "half step into the future." Children's minds, he said, work differently from adults', and a hit is something that resonates with children (Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima). So the number kills what is not working, and the hypothesis decides what to try next. Veto by data; direction by judgment. Every product organization that has ever argued about metrics versus vision is re-litigating a question a manga editor settled in 1980.

### What the no produced

Watch the system work. In 1980 Toriyama brought Torishima a comic about an inventor named Senbei Norimaki. Torishima said the lead should be the inventor's robot girl, Arale. Toriyama's first response, verbatim: "I don't wanna." He did it anyway, and later agreed it "turned out better" (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews; Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima). *Dr. Slump* ran from 1980 to 1984, was an immediate hit, took the Shogakukan Manga Award in 1981, and became an anime on Fuji TV the same year. Toriyama drew it without an assistant, on about an hour of sleep a day; there are, he said, "about two chapters I don't even remember inking" (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews). When he drew a villain and the villain came back generic, the editor's note was not "fix it." It was: "He has no unique traits. Make him even worse." The villain became Dr. Mashirito, whose name is Torishima's own syllables reversed. The artist put his editor in the comic as the bad guy, and the editor, by the account of his own book, embraced it.

When Toriyama begged to end *Dr. Slump*, the no came with a condition instead of a number: "If you start a new serial three months later, then you can end it" (Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No). Torishima knew his artist had watched *Drunken Master* dozens of times, so he suggested a kung-fu comic. He suggested *Journey to the West* as the frame, because it was free to use and a Chinese setting was unusual in manga and far from *Dr. Slump*'s American-West-Coast feel (Kanzenshuu, Daizenshuu 2; Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima). Toriyama tested the idea as a one-shot called *Dragon Boy* in 1983 and then, in 1984, began *Dragon Ball*. When its early chapters underperformed, Torishima told him the protagonist was "rather plain." The remark annoyed Toriyama. It also led to the martial-arts tournament format that he later credited with the series' rise (Kanzenshuu, Daizenshuu 2). Note what the no did each time. It did not add. It redirected. The artist's stubbornness supplied the energy; the editor's instrument supplied the direction; the survey supplied the proof.

### Three words on a page

Jobs's numbered no came from a different quarter and in a different shape. It came from Mike Markkula, the investor who in 1977 wrote "The Apple Marketing Philosophy," a document built on three words: Empathy, Focus, Impute. Empathy meant understanding what the customer needs. Focus meant eliminating distractions. Impute meant that the way a product is presented must have the same quality and rigor as the product itself, because the presentation is the first thing the customer reads (Steve Jobs Archive, "The Apple Marketing Philosophy"). Decades later, explaining why the tactile experience of opening an iPhone box should set the tone for the whole product, Jobs added three more words: "Mike taught me that."

Three words are not a survey. But they function the same way, as a test the creator cannot talk his way around. Is this feature a distraction? Then Focus says no. Does the box look like an afterthought? Then Impute says no. The system did not depend on anyone's mood, and it outlived the man who wrote it.

He had received a numbered no himself in 1979, when CEO Mike Scott would not let him run the Lisa and he moved to the smaller Macintosh team instead (Computer History Museum; Britannica). The ones he went on to give form a pattern that any list makes obvious. No to Sony's "high-tech" surface styling, which he told the Aspen conference in 1983 was "easy to do that. But it's not great" (Smithsonian/Isaacson). No to a fan in the iMac: "I was adamant that we get rid of the fan" (Fortune, 2000). No to the board's compensation: "The board has made several incredibly generous offers. I have turned them all down" (Fortune, 2000). And the no that every Macintosh user has typed through without knowing it.

### An evening in August 1983

Susan Kare had joined the Macintosh group to draw bitmaps. She liked drawing screen fonts in particular because the Mac, unlike the monospace machines of the day with their "narrow m's and wide i's," could show proportional type. Her first face was a bold system font with no jagged diagonals, which she called Elefont. She and Andy Hertzfeld had gone to high school together outside Philadelphia, so they named the rest after stops on the Paoli Local commuter line: Overbrook, Merion, Ardmore, Rosemont. One, built from mismatched letters like a kidnapper's cut-out note, was called Ransom (Folklore.org, "World Class Cities").

Jobs came through the software group one evening, looked at the font menu, and frowned.

> "What are those names?" … "cities are OK, but not little cities that nobody's ever heard of. They ought to be WORLD CLASS cities!"
> — Steve Jobs, August 1983, as recounted by Susan Kare (Folklore.org)

Elefont became Chicago. Ransom became San Francisco. The rest became New York, Geneva, London, Toronto, and, for Bill Atkinson's chancery-cursive script, Venice. This is the only typographic decision on the original Macintosh with a first-person witness account tying it to Jobs. He did not draw a single pixel of those fonts; Kare and Atkinson drew them, and Atkinson's QuickDraw made proportional text possible at all (Folklore.org; Wikipedia: List of Apple typefaces). What Jobs contributed was a no, applied to the one thing in the room that was not yet world-class. Impute, in action, on an ordinary evening. Thirty-two years later, after his death, Apple would name its new system typeface San Francisco and, four years after that, its serif companion New York (Wikipedia: Typography of Apple Inc.). The no outlived the man who said it, which is how you know it was a system and not a mood.

### 1984, twice

The two timelines reach their first shared landmark in the same month. On January 22, 1984, a Ridley Scott commercial that cost a million and a half dollars aired once during the Super Bowl. On January 24, at the shareholders' meeting, the Macintosh was introduced, and one of the first images on its screen was a 1920 woodblock print by Hashiguchi Goyō, *Woman Combing Her Hair*, which Kare had digitized from a scan Atkinson made of a print hanging in Jobs's own home (Britannica; Wikipedia: Steve Jobs; nobi.com; Smithsonian NMAA). A Japanese print, the medium that taught a nation to think in flat color and bounding line, was the demonstration image for the first mass-market bitmap display. Jobs had started collecting shin-hanga prints only ten months earlier, after walking into a gallery in Ginza with Steve Wozniak and Rod Holt in March 1983, where the dealer "had no idea who they were" (JREF reproduction of NHK synopsis; Bijutsu Techo).

The same year, in Tokyo, *Dr. Slump* ended and *Dragon Ball* began. Toriyama had made the hero's clothing deliberately plain, because it was a fighting story. He had given him a tail because, he said, "I should give him a bit of a defining characteristic" (Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No). He had first planned to make him an actual monkey and decided that was "not very innovative" (Kanzenshuu, Daizenshuu 2). Plain clothes, one tail, a boy. The design brief was already the compression brief: one distinguishing mark on an otherwise ordinary silhouette.

Both products had been forced through a numbered no. Both shipped in 1984. Neither man, so far as any record shows, had heard of the other.

## Part V: The Worst-Screen Rule (1984–1986)

### A monster that suffocates you

The brief arrived as a sentence. "No fixed shape. A gelatinous, goopy monster. It attaches itself to people's faces, etc. and suffocates them" (Kanzenshuu wiki, Dragon Quest). The man who wrote it, Yuji Horii, was designing a role-playing game for the Famicom and had borrowed the creature from the computer game *Wizardry*, where it was, in his words, "a pile of goo." He doodled it and sent the doodle to an artist he had been told was eager for the job.

The artist was not eager. Toriyama had been pushed into it by Torishima, who wanted a video game that *Jump* could cover exclusively and had pitched first the game and then the artist; Horii was told Toriyama wanted the work, which Horii learned later was only half true (Kanzenshuu wiki, Dragon Quest; Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima). Toriyama himself remembered receiving "the gloopy slime monster that basically looked like a puddle" and nearly declining the whole thing: "…I wasn't all that knowledgeable about fantasy worlds in the first place" (RPGFan). He took the job for the money and because Torishima insisted.

What he did next is the clearest record of the compression instinct at work, because the input and the output both survive. He drew a pixel-art version first and threw it away, because it did not look like his. Then he sent back a teardrop. A single closed contour, pointed at the top, round at the bottom, with two dots for eyes and one curve for a mouth. Horii looked at it and called it "perfect." He named the game's monsters only after seeing Toriyama's drawings of them (Wikipedia: Slime (Dragon Quest)). *Dragon Quest* shipped on May 27, 1986, with Toriyama hired, in the publisher's reasoning, to appeal to a wider audience than the genre had reached (Wikipedia: Dragon Quest (video game)).

Horror became cuteness. A thing with no shape became the most recognizable shape in Japanese games. A face-hugger became a face. The brief was inverted at every point, and the inversion was not whimsy; it was what the pipe demanded. A Famicom sprite is a handful of pixels. A monster with no fixed shape cannot be drawn in a handful of pixels, because "no fixed shape" is the one thing a tiny grid cannot express. So the artist gave it the most fixed shape there is, a symmetrical contour with a point for orientation, and put the minimum facial grammar inside it. Toriyama stated the rule himself, years later, as a sprite-era principle:

> "You just have to give them a design with a feature that you can tell 'it's this character' even when they're small."
> — Akira Toriyama, Daizenshuu 5 (Kanzenshuu wiki, Dragon Quest)

### The third law

That sentence is the third law of the compression instinct. Design for the worst screen the thing will ever appear on, and design for it first. Call it the worst-screen rule.

Most designers do the opposite. They draw the hero image at full size, in full color, with every detail, and then "scale it down" for the icon, the favicon, the thumbnail, the sprite, and are surprised when it turns to mud. The worst-screen rule says the small version is not a derivative of the large one. The small version is the identity. The large version is the identity with decoration added. If you cannot tell it's this character when it is sixteen pixels tall, then you do not yet have a character; you have a drawing.

The physics is simple. Every rendering surface is a channel with a capacity. The smallest surface has the lowest capacity, and whatever gets through it is, by definition, the lowest-entropy description of the thing that still works. Find that description and you have found the thing's signal. Everything you add afterward is either redundancy, which is harmless, or noise, which is not. The Slime is a signal with almost nothing else attached, which is why one games writer observed that it is "so simple it borders on effortless," and why it has appeared in nearly every entry of the series since 1986 "with little change" (RPGFan). Ask a Japanese person to draw "a slime," a GameSpy critic noted, and the typical result is the onion shape of this weakest enemy in a 1986 game (GameSpy, archived). That is a logo. One contour, no interior detail, indifferent to scale, unchanged for four decades, and the thing a nation draws when asked for the brand.

Horii had his own theory of why it stuck, and it is a product theory rather than an art theory: cuteness, ease of defeat, and constant presence while the protagonists change (Wikipedia: Slime (Dragon Quest)). The mascot is the one element that is not redesigned when the cast is. Square Enix today runs an entire "Smile Slime" product line, and a Slime-themed PlayStation 4 sold about thirty-eight thousand units in Japan (Wikipedia: Slime (Dragon Quest)). A teardrop drawn reluctantly for extra income in 1986 is still paying.

### 128 kilobytes and a one-bit screen

The Macintosh was designed under the same rule two years earlier, with a worse screen. The machine had 128 kilobytes of memory and a display that could show each pixel as black or white and nothing else. There was no grey. There was no anti-aliasing. A diagonal line was a staircase. Susan Kare's answer was Chicago: a bold system face with no jagged diagonals, because on a one-bit screen a diagonal is exactly the thing that fails (Folklore.org). Chicago stayed the Macintosh system font until Mac OS 8 and then reappeared, more than a decade and a half after it was drawn, on early iPods, whose small screens were another floor (Wikipedia: List of Apple typefaces). A face designed for the floor of 1984 was ready for a pocket in the 2000s without a single change.

Jobs understood the other half of the rule, which is that the floor is not the ceiling. In December 1983, a month before the Mac was announced, he convinced John Warnock of Adobe to license PostScript to Apple, and Apple bought $2.5 million of Adobe stock. The LaserWriter that resulted was announced on January 23, 1985, at $6,995, with a 300-dot-per-inch Canon engine, four built-in PostScript faces, and AppleTalk networking that let up to sixteen Macs share one printer and brought the cost per user under $450. Aldus announced PageMaker the same day, and a venture firm coined the phrase "desktop publishing" (Wikipedia: LaserWriter). PostScript fonts are outlines, not bitmaps; they describe a letter as a shape rather than as a grid of dots, so the same description prints at any size. On screen, the Mac had Kare's bitmaps, drawn for the worst case. On paper, it had outlines, drawn for every case. The system worked at both ends because the identity had been established at the bottom and only then allowed to scale. Reed's own history of its calligraphy program names Apple and Adobe as the two companies through which the Reynolds tradition's "sensibility about letterforms" spread into the computer interface (Reed College, "The Calligraphy Heritage at Reed"). A monk's italic hand had become a printer driver.

### The constraint is the specification

Every number in this story is tiny on purpose, and it is worth laying them side by side so the pattern cannot be missed. One hundred twenty-eight kilobytes. One bit per pixel. Fourteen pages a week. One assistant. A $6,995 printer that had to pay for itself across sixteen users. A sprite a few pixels tall. And, soon, a grid of twelve by twelve.

None of the people in this story negotiated their constraint away. They treated it as the specification, and the constraint became the style. Chicago has no diagonals because the screen could not draw them, and no diagonals became Macintosh. Toriyama worked in solid blacks because he could not afford screentone, and solid blacks became *Dragon Ball*. The Slime is a teardrop because a Famicom could not draw a puddle, and the teardrop became *Dragon Quest*. In each case the designer could have pleaded for a better pipe. Each one instead found out what fit through the pipe he had, and what fit turned out to be the only part anyone remembers. The constraint is not the obstacle to the identity. It is the instrument that reveals it.

### 1985: the showman learns the no from the other side

Between the Mac and the Slime, Jobs lost his company. In 1985 John Sculley, the chief executive Jobs had recruited two years earlier, persuaded the board to remove him from operations; Jobs submitted a resignation letter on September 17 (Britannica; Wikipedia: Steve Jobs). He called it being fired, and said so at Stanford twenty years later, "How can you get fired from a company you started?", and his word is the one that stuck, though formally it was a resignation. He was thirty. He founded NeXT, invited Kōbun to be its "spiritual advisor," and in 1986 bought a controlling interest in Pixar from Lucasfilm for ten million dollars (Nippon.com; Britannica; Wikipedia: Steve Jobs).

The first thing he commissioned for the new company is a small lesson in the worst-screen rule from a master of it. He hired Paul Rand to design NeXT's logo for a flat fee of a hundred thousand dollars. Rand delivered a black cube with the company's name broken across two lines and the "e" in lowercase, and presented it in a privately printed booklet. Jobs said Rand had given them "a jewel, which in retrospect seems so obvious" (RR Auction; Wikipedia: Paul Rand). *So obvious* is what the signal looks like once the noise is gone. A decade later, shortly before Rand died, Jobs called him "the greatest living graphic designer." The man who could not draw a font had learned, from a monk, an investor, a Zen priest, and now a logo designer, what the irreducible version of a thing looks like. He would spend the next twenty-five years demanding it.

## Part VI: The Convenience Gradient (1986–1995)

### The hair nobody inked

Two people are inking in a room in Kiyosu. One of them is Toriyama. The other is his single assistant, who blacks in areas. The pages are *Dragon Ball*, the comic that will push its magazine to a record circulation, and the pipeline is fourteen pages a week, every week, which the artist will later describe as "very hard" and something he "really didn't like" (Kanzenshuu, Shonen Jump 2003). Goku has black hair, and black hair is a large solid area that has to be filled and left to dry. In the early 1990s the hero transforms for the first time into something called a Super Saiyan, and his hair changes. In the color version it is blond. In the black-and-white magazine, where the story actually lived, it is simply left blank.

Thirty years later, asked about it by his old editor, Toriyama explained:

> "Initially, I was fairly particular about the inking, but then I gradually found ways to save on time." … "That's because the first person to finish inking would have to be the one to erase all the pencil marks."
> — Akira Toriyama (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews)

Torishima's reply, in the same conversation: "it's cutting corners, but I suppose the readers won't notice." Toriyama's verdict on himself: "I'm a master of the plausible excuse." In the same interview he admitted that he had destroyed the ring of the Tenkaichi Budokai tournament "because it seemed like a pain to draw." A later *Jump* editor-in-chief, Hiroyuki Nakano, confirmed on television that the blond hair was about eliminating the time spent coloring in black, because for a weekly serial "each minute and every second is precious" (Den of Geek). Torishima's summary of his artist: "a master of convenience," and "sloppy, but in a good way" (Wikipedia: Akira Toriyama).

This is the material from which the laziness story is built, and the material is real. What the laziness story misses is that the uninked hair is the most legible power-up in the history of comics. Black became white. On a page full of black, the hero now glows. The shortcut was not merely tolerated by the reader; it *was* the visual grammar of transformation, and it cost nothing.

### The fourth law

The fourth law of the compression instinct is a rule about where effort goes. Economize where the reader will not notice. Escalate where the reader will. Call it the convenience gradient: a slope that runs from the parts of the work that carry no signal, where you should spend nothing, to the parts that carry all of it, where you spend everything you saved.

Toriyama stated both halves. The first half he stated as a confession, in July 1995, in the most honest sentence any designer has ever given an interviewer:

> "Basically with everything, I choose my criteria based on what can be easy."
> — Akira Toriyama, Daizenshuu 4, July 24, 1995 (Kanzenshuu)

He then itemized. He built a fictional world rather than a real one because "If I made the real world the setting, I'd have to draw looking at reference materials, for stuff like buildings and vehicles," and "people complain even if it's just a little bit off." He gave his characters the power of flight because "Up until then, they had to bring out planes and automobiles and the like one by one, and it was really tiresome," after which "the story's progress became speedy," since "everyone became able to just fly straight to anywhere." He chose *Journey to the West* as a frame partly because "I thought it would be easy if that story served as the basis" (Kanzenshuu, Daizenshuu 4; Daizenshuu 2). He did not plot ahead, and when Torishima observed, "You don't take any time thinking about the story, after all," Toriyama agreed he "simply didn't do it because it was a pain" (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews).

Now read every one of those as engineering instead of confession. An invented world eliminates reference errors, so the shortcut doubles as world-building freedom. Flight compresses travel time, so the shortcut doubles as pacing; it is the comics equivalent of a film editor cutting the drive to the airport. Blank hair is a stronger graphic signal than inked hair, so the shortcut doubles as iconography. Plain clothes make the silhouette carry the identity, so the shortcut doubles as a brand. Not plotting ahead produces what Torishima prized in his artist's unplanned pages and called *uneri*, a dynamic swell (Kanzenshuu forum, Torishima book summary). Each saving removed a recurring cost from a weekly pipeline while preserving or increasing what the reader received. That is not laziness. That is a compression algorithm running on a human being.

### The other half of the slope

The second half of the law is what separates compression from mere cheapness, and Toriyama stated it just as plainly:

> "If you draw it on the same level as the previous fight, it'll seem to the readers that the level has actually gone down."
> — Akira Toriyama (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews)

Backgrounds are randomized; a critic described his landscapes as "generally randomized," interchangeable sets behind the choreography, and Toriyama himself said that for repeated tournaments he changed "the shapes of the rocks or the mountains in the distance" because "having the same scenery is no good" (4thletter; Kanzenshuu, Daizenshuu 4). But fights escalate, because the fight is the signal. One essayist marveled that Toriyama achieves "such an intense sensation of action within the boundaries of a single manga frame" (Kanzenshuu, Toriyama Exhibition Essays). The frame could afford that intensity because the rocks behind it had cost nothing.

He also rebuilt his whole drawing hand to serve the slope. After studying the designs of Toyo'o Ashida, the animator who adapted *Dr. Slump* for television, Toriyama said "my own speed also rose by about a factor of 15," and concluded his earlier rounded style "wasn't really suited to the movement of action" (Kanzenshuu, Dr. Mashirito interviews). Fifteen times faster is not a figure of speech from a man who measured his life in pages per week. The savings were reinvested where the reader looked: Horie's essay notes that *Dragon Ball* "effectively uses multiple streamlines to express speed and action," and a critic praised panel-to-panel storytelling that was "immaculate as ever" (Kanzenshuu, Toriyama Exhibition Essays; 4thletter). The lines got fewer and faster, and the fewer lines were all in the right place.

### Simple can be harder than complex

Jobs ran the same gradient and described it in the opposite moral register. Where Toriyama said "easy," Jobs said "hard," and they meant the same operation.

> "That's been one of my mantras — focus and simplicity. Simple can be harder than complex: You have to work hard to get your thinking clean to make it simple. But it's worth it in the end because once you get there, you can move mountains."
> — Steve Jobs, *BusinessWeek*, 1998 (reproduced by Inc.)

"Get your thinking clean" is the top of the slope: the effort spent finding the signal. "Move mountains" is the bottom: what you can do once everything else has been removed. The iMac of 1998 is the object lesson. Its lead designer was Jony Ive, but the decision Jobs reported owning was a removal: "I was adamant that we get rid of the fan" (Fortune, 2000). A fan is noise in both senses. Removing it was economy where the user would not notice the absence and would notice the silence.

Then the reinvestment. Markkula's Impute said the presentation deserved the rigor of the product, and Jobs spent the saved budget on the box. The tactile experience of opening it, he said, should set the tone for how you perceive what is inside (Steve Jobs Archive, "The Apple Marketing Philosophy"). Toriyama randomized the rocks and escalated the fight; Jobs removed the fan and lavished the packaging. Same slope, opposite accents.

By 2000 Jobs had sharpened the point into a definition. "In most people's vocabularies, design means veneer," he told *Fortune* (Fortune, 2000). In November 2003, to the *New York Times Magazine*:

> "Most people make the mistake of thinking design is what it looks like… People think it's this veneer — that the designers are handed this box and told, 'Make it look good!' That's not what we think design is. It's not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works."
> — Steve Jobs, *New York Times Magazine*, November 30, 2003 (via Harvard blogs reproduction)

Ive, who inherited the job, put the cost of it plainly: "Simplicity isn't just a visual style… To be truly simple, you have to go really deep" (Smithsonian/Isaacson). Going deep is the price of finding out which parts are the signal. It is exactly the work Toriyama described as "finding ways to save on time," seen from the other side of the ledger.

### Why the two accounts differ

It matters that the showman moralized the gradient and the hermit apologized for it, because the difference tells you where the pressure came from. Jobs chose his narrow pipe, and so could present the choice as virtue. Toriyama's pipe was imposed by a magazine and a survey and a calendar, and so he presented his compliance as cutting corners. Underneath, the operation was identical: a cost-removal exercise with a quality floor, in which simplification must never reduce the signal, only the noise around it. Both men were describing the moment when the thing has nothing left to remove.

Toriyama ended *Dragon Ball* in 1995 after 519 chapters in 42 volumes; the magazine's circulation had peaked that year at a record 6.53 million copies (Wikipedia: Akira Toriyama). He wrote in the final volume that he wanted to take some new steps in life, and said eighteen years later, more directly, "I wanted to quit because I was exhausted" (Kanzenshuu, Men's Non-No). The same year, in California, a company Jobs had bought for ten million dollars released *Toy Story*, the first fully computer-animated feature film, and its stock offering made him a billionaire (Britannica). One man had just finished the work that defined him. The other was about to be handed back the company that had thrown him out. And in Tokyo, inside the mobile-phone business, something was about to be drawn on a grid of twelve by twelve that neither of them would ever comment on and both of them had, in different ways, made possible.

## Part VII: The Empty Face

### A flying wing on a comic-book cover

Look at the covers of the collected *Dr. Slump*. The cast, a robot girl and the villagers around her, is drawn in the broadest cartoon shorthand: circles for eyes, a curve for a mouth. And they are posed, volume after volume, on real Second World War aircraft, rendered with the accuracy of a man who had built the kits. Later the *Dragon Ball* chapter title pages continued the habit with vehicles. The subjects were not famous; they were obscure and correct. A Reliant Scimitar SS1. The Horten Ho 229, a German flying wing. Custom pickups on a Mercedes chassis for Master Roshi. In a 1986 one-shot, hovercars with the bodies of a Ferrari Dino and a Porsche 356. In *Sand Land*, a tank whose turret mixes a Somua S35 and an AMX 40 on treads like a Crusader, trucks like old Citroëns, an off-road car like a Kübelwagen (Zimmerit). An automotive writer called him "an unabashed gearhead" who draws his characters "crammed" into a Suzuki Jimny, a Fiat Abarth, a Porsche 911, and gave his heroine a capsule motorbike that resembles a BMW C1 (Jalopnik).

Toriyama had joked about this habit in 1983, before *Dragon Ball* existed, by naming the heroine of a one-shot "Plamo," which is Japanese shorthand for plastic model (Zimmerit). He never stopped. In the author's note to *Sand Land* he admitted, "But the tank was harder to draw than I expected," and that he "stubbornly insisted on drawing it all myself." Here is the man who chose everything by what could be easy, who gave his characters flight so he would never draw another car, refusing to delegate a tank.

Why? Because the machines were never where the reader lived. The reader lived in the face.

### The fifth law

The fifth law of the compression instinct is the one that explains why these objects cross cultures without translation, and it comes out of comics theory. The fewer marks a face has, the more people it can be. Call it the empty face.

Scott McCloud's *Understanding Comics* made the argument in 1993. A simply drawn, "iconic" character lets readers project themselves into the story; the character works as a mask the reader wears. We look to eyes and mouths for emotion, so a minimal face leaves room for the reader's own feeling. And McCloud identified a specific configuration he called the masking effect, iconic characters set against realistically rendered backgrounds and objects, which for a time was "nearly a national style" in Japan: in boys' comics the antagonists are often drawn more realistically than the simple hero, and props are drawn simply until the moment they are shown in realistic detail to mark them as objects, things in the world rather than selves (Wikipedia: Masking (comics), summarizing McCloud pp. 42–44).

Toriyama is the textbook case, though no scholar has written the textbook. A two-dot Slime. A circle-eyed boy. And, behind them, a Horten flying wing rendered from a model kit. The empty face is where you go in. The accurate machine is what tells you the world is solid. One essayist in the 2013 exhibition catalogue, the copywriter Shigesato Itoi, put the abstraction half of this precisely: manga is closer to abstract art than to representation; "a circle with a dot in the middle stands for an eye in manga"; and Toriyama's world is itself non-representational, "a delight to millions of readers" (Kanzenshuu, Toriyama Exhibition Essays). Another, Hideto Fuse, called his figure work a "'manga-fication' of the aesthetics of Michelangelo," which is the other half: real anatomy inside a cartoon contour (Kanzenshuu, Toriyama Exhibition Essays).

In information terms, the empty face is a deliberate gap in the signal: the sender leaves a region of the message blank so that the receiver fills it with his own data. A photorealistic face carries one person's identity and can be only that person. A face with two dots carries no one's and can be anyone's. The less you transmit, the more the receiver supplies, and what the receiver supplies is himself. This is why the Slime's smile makes players, in one critic's words, hesitate to kill it (Wikipedia: Slime (Dragon Quest)). They are being asked to kill a face they have partly drawn.

### The learned alphabet

But the empty face is only half of the explanation, and the other half is a correction. Not everything in a Toriyama page is pre-linguistic. A great deal of it is a language, and languages have to be learned.

Neil Cohn, a cognitive scientist who studies comics as language, and Sean Ehly catalogued seventy-three conventionalized graphic schemas across a twenty-book corpus of manga in a 2016 paper: the nosebleed for lust, the nose bubble for sleep, the sweat drop, the anger mark. They argued these form a "visual vocabulary" within a single "Japanese Visual Language," whose boys' and girls' genres are dialects that differ in proportion rather than in kind (Tilburg University record of Cohn and Ehly, *Journal of Pragmatics*, 2016). Cohn's laboratory work goes further. Using *Peanuts* strips stripped of dialogue, he and colleagues found that scrambling the panel order produces the same brain response, a left-anterior negativity, that an ungrammatical sentence produces. "Sequential images have a grammar like sequential words do," Cohn said. "The brain is processing these different kinds of grammars in a common way" (Carl Zimmer, *Discover*).

So a Toriyama page is read fast partly because readers of *Jump* had already learned the alphabet: speed lines mean motion, a drop means nervousness, a burst means impact. McCloud's universality claim has been criticized on exactly this point, that "simplicity" is culturally subjective and that readers may identify through plot rather than design (Wikipedia: Masking (comics)). The criticism is fair, and it does not sink the law. It splits it.

### Two alphabets, two speeds

Every one of the objects in this story has two layers, and the layers operate at different speeds. The first is a learned vocabulary: local, conventional, acquired by exposure. The second is a pre-linguistic shape: a silhouette, a contour, two dots and a curve, which needs no instruction at all. The winning move, in every case, was to keep the learned layer small and conventional and to put the entire identity of the thing into the pre-linguistic layer.

Toriyama's story is *Journey to the West*, a tale his readers already knew; his vocabulary is standard manga shorthand; and all his originality is in the silhouette, the plain-clothed boy with a tail, the teardrop with a face. Kare's Geneva was, in effect, "a redesigned Helvetica," the most conventional sans-serif alive, and the Mac's identity sat in the icons and the bold diagonal-free Chicago (Wikipedia: Typography of Apple Inc.; Folklore.org). A proportionally spaced serif is a learned vocabulary with a five-hundred-year grammar; a single round button on the front of a phone is a face. Products become unreadable when they do the opposite: a novel vocabulary and a novel shape at once, so that the user must learn two things to see one.

There is a test for whether the identity has been put in the right layer, and a French critic supplied it: "Akira Toriyama's drawings do not lose anything when reversed" (Jean-Paul Jennequin, Kanzenshuu, Toriyama Exhibition Essays). Flip the page. If it still works, the composition is structural, not habitual; the identity is in the shape, not in a convention of reading direction. Logo designers run the same check on a mark. Toriyama's rule that a character must be identifiable "even when they're small," Kare's refusal of diagonals, and Jennequin's reversal test are three ways of enforcing one requirement: the signal must survive a change of channel.

### The export of an alphabet

Here the two layers stop being a theory and become a date. The learned alphabet of manga, the sweat drop and the light bulb and the anger mark, was local to Japan in 1990. By 2015 the Oxford Dictionaries could name a pictograph its Word of the Year and be understood everywhere (InfoDocket). What happened in between was that a telephone company compiled the alphabet into a character set, an American phone put the character set on a keyboard, and the whole world learned the Japanese dialect in about a decade. The man who drew the set would say that overseas use "really took off starting from 2012" (CNN Style).

That export is where the two lineages, the showman's and the hermit's, finally touch the same object. Notice first what made it possible. The sweat drop could be learned because it was tiny and conventional. The face could be understood without learning because it was empty. A character set that contained both could be adopted by a culture that knew neither, provided it was drawn small enough. It was drawn on a grid of twelve by twelve.

## Part VIII: Eighteen Bytes (1995–2016)

### Five weeks, 176 ideas, no focus groups

Tokyo, 1998 or 1999. A man in his mid-twenties is drawing on a grid. He works for NTT DoCoMo, which is about to launch a mobile internet service called i-mode, and the service has a problem: its emails are capped at 250 characters, and the handsets' specifications, in his words, are "really poor." You cannot say much in 250 characters. You cannot say *how you feel* in 250 characters without wasting most of them. The man, Shigetaka Kurita, born May 9, 1972, in Gifu Prefecture, has been given about five weeks to solve this with pictures (Wikipedia: Shigetaka Kurita; CNN Style; Emojipedia, "Correcting the Record").

The pictures have a size: twelve pixels by twelve. That is 144 pixels, each one on or off. Eighteen bytes per glyph. The full set of 176 comes to just over three kilobytes (CNN Style). Three kilobytes is about a page of plain text. Kurita disliked the grid, and the reason he disliked it is the kind of reason only a designer would have: "I didn't like it, because the number of spaces in the grid was not an odd number." Twelve is even. On an even grid there is no center pixel, so nothing can be perfectly symmetrical, and several of the original glyphs are visibly, slightly off-center as a result. The co-founder of the firm that republished the set as a book in 2018 said that designing 176 ideas into twelve-pixel symbols in five weeks would make most designers "faint at the idea," and that Kurita's team made every decision without focus groups or testing (CNN Style; It's Nice That).

Where did the vocabulary come from? Kurita has answered this many times, and the answer has been widely misreported. He drew on pictograms, on weather symbols, on street signs, on the Zapf Dingbats typeface, on emoticons, and above all on manga and its symbolic marks, the *manpu*, "such as a water drop for nervousness" (Wikipedia: Shigetaka Kurita; MoMA). He was explicit about what was not a source:

> "In creating emoji, I found inspiration in pictograms, manga, and all sorts of other sources." … "Both emoji and kanji are ideograms, but I did not find inspiration for designing emoji in the kanji."
> — Shigetaka Kurita (CNN Style)

So the "kanji inspired emoji" line that appears in half the popular histories is wrong by the designer's own testimony. Kanji are an analogy. Manga is the source. Kurita compiled the learned alphabet of Japanese comics, the one Cohn would later catalogue at seventy-three schemas, into a character set small enough to fit through a 250-character pipe. Then the other carriers did what carriers do. "Every single one just took what we had and implemented it the way it was" (Emojipedia, "Correcting the Record").

One glyph in the set was older than the rest. In 1995 DoCoMo had added a heart symbol to its Pocket Bell pagers, and Kurita had been on that team; the red heart carried over and has carried over ever since, through every standard and every vendor, and it remains his favorite, "because among the various emoji, (its meaning) is very positive" (Wikipedia: Shigetaka Kurita; CNN Style). One glyph he wanted was refused. He asked for a pile of poo. "NTT DoCoMo (said) 'no good,' and I wasn't able to create it" (CNN Style). Hold that refusal. It will decide where the world's poop comes from.

### The set that came first

For most of the 2010s the story ended there: Kurita invented emoji in 1999. The Museum of Modern Art acquired his 176 glyphs in October 2016, as object 961.2016, a gift of NTT DOCOMO, and exhibited them as *Inbox: The Original Emoji* (MoMA; Wikipedia: Emoji). The story was wrong, in the way that stories about origins usually are: the first was not the first.

On November 1, 1997, J-Phone, the carrier later known as SoftBank, released a handset called the SkyWalker DP-211SW. It carried ninety emoji, monochrome, on the same twelve-by-twelve grid. The handset sold poorly. Its designers are unknown. And among its ninety glyphs was a smiling pile of poo, drawn in black and white with a smile and steam lines (Emojipedia, "Correcting the Record"; Wikipedia: Emoji; Wikipedia: Poop emoji). The evidence was assembled piece by piece, from a 2017 image tracing, a paper manual found by a user and recreated pixel for pixel, and a 2016 conference talk that questioned the DoCoMo story. On January 3, 2019, Kurita himself tweeted that the DP-211SW was likely Japan's first emoji phone, and on March 8, 2019, Emojipedia's Jeremy Burge published a piece titled "Correcting the Record on the First Emoji Set" (Emojipedia).

Two facts in that correction matter more than the question of who was first. The 1997 set contained the poop. And Apple's emoji descend from SoftBank's set, while Google's descend from the set of the carrier au by KDDI (Emojipedia, "Correcting the Record"). DoCoMo never had a poop, because DoCoMo said no. So when, a decade later, an American phone company needed an emoji font for Japan and built it on its Japanese partner's encoding, the swirl it inherited came from a handset nobody bought, drawn by nobody anyone can name. MoMA still catalogues the work as "Shigetaka Kurita. Emoji. 1998–1999," and the choice is defensible on its own terms: Kurita's is the set the industry copied and the only one with a named designer. But the lineage of the single most argued-over glyph in the set runs around him, not through him.

### 💩 The most useful emoji

Mountain View and Tokyo, 2007 to 2008. Google has partnered with KDDI au to bring emoji to Gmail, under an internal project name, "Mojo," and has offered to fix a problem that has plagued Japan for a decade: the three carriers' emoji use mismatched code points, so a glyph sent from one network can arrive as a different glyph on another (Fast Company, "The Oral History of the Poop Emoji"). The American lead on the project, Darick Tong, wants a bigger Google presence in Japan and Asia. The project takes longer than anyone planned. A Google engineer, Darren Lewis, remembers colleagues dismissing the whole thing as "stupid animated emoticons," and marketing refusing to use the word "emoji" at all (Fast Company).

Then the fight over the poop. Some people at headquarters found an animated pile of excrement offensive and saw emoji as an invasion of email's "purity." Including it, they argued, would complicate the system and delay the launch. The team countered with data: it was among the most popular emoji in the Japanese usage they had analyzed. It was harder to justify outside Japan. Takeshi Kishimoto, Google's Japanese product manager, insisted it be in the first release, and, by his own account, got the Gmail product manager to agree that it was the most useful emoji (Fast Company; Wikipedia: Poop emoji). Asked years later why it mattered so much, Kishimoto reached for a comic. The character, he said, had gained its popularity through the 1980s comedy animation *Dr. Slump*, where it was a playful prop and was "never serious." Kat Momoi, a Google test engineer, added the linguistic half: *unchi* is a benign child's word in Japanese, and its meanings turned negative only when the glyph spread outside Japan (Fast Company).

The drawing was done by the Google Doodle team, Ryan Germick and Susie Sahim, at fifteen by fifteen pixels, in Google's four brand colors, with one color per glyph to keep the files small; Sahim did most of the faces and Germick most of the objects (Fast Company). Germick added circling flies to the poop, called it his greatest contribution and the Gmail poop his "proudest achievement," compared designing it on the spot to Chaplin inventing the Tramp, and described how people actually use it: as "the anti-like" (Fast Company; Boing Boing). After launch, Lewis hid an animated version in Gchat, submitting the code under a deliberately boring review title so no one would look; typing a tilde, an at-sign, and a tilde made it swirl (Fast Company). Tong's verdict on the glyph was that it was "a particularly flexible and effective emoji." Momoi's verdict on emoji in general was that they can be "the easiest way to apologize—words can't express enough" (Fast Company).

Gmail's emoji launched in October 2008. About a month later, Apple's did.

### Documented, primed, mythologized

Now the question that gives this story its internet afterlife: did Akira Toriyama invent the poop emoji? The honest answer requires separating three links of very different strength.

The first link is documented. In *Dr. Slump*, which ran from 1980 to 1984, there is a recurring character called Unchi-kun, Poop-Boy, "one of several coiled feces that look much like soft ice cream," who has "a sense of justice" and feet for running, who gets an origin story in chapter 178 (first born when the inventor Senbei needed a roadside bathroom), and who befriends Bird-Poop-Boy and Manure-Boy. He was voiced in the 1981 anime and again in 1997, and appears briefly in the *Dragon Ball* anime (Wikipedia: List of Dr. Slump characters). Arale, the robot girl, "is known for poking poop as a hobby"; in chapter 19 of *Dragon Ball* a character named Mai holds a poop on a stick (Dragon Ball Wiki). One magazine described the comic as "full of puns and bathroom jokes, with living, pastel-colored poops that walk, talk, and grin" (Dazed). Also documented: the 1997 J-Phone glyph; DoCoMo's refusal; the Google fight; Kishimoto's invocation of *Dr. Slump* on the record in November 2014; and Unicode's encoding of U+1F4A9 PILE OF POO in version 6.0 in October 2010 (Wikipedia: Emoji; Wikipedia: Poop emoji).

The second link is cultural priming, and it is real but diffuse. The idea that feces "had first taken on humorous connotations" in early-1980s Japan after *Dr. Slump* appears in the encyclopedia entry on the emoji, but that sentence is uncited, and the claim that Google's designers drew on Poop-Boy names no designer (Wikipedia: Poop emoji). Japan supplied plenty of priming on its own. *Unko* begins with the same sound as the word for luck. Households kept privy-god figures, *banjo-gami*, near the toilet. Around 2000 a Kyoto company began selling golden poop charms, Kin no Unko, and the object became "a popular, humorous, positive object for children, without Western stigmas attached" (Dazed; Wikipedia: Poop emoji). The golden-charm boom postdates the 1997 glyph, so it cannot have caused it, though the pun is older. A poop glyph even existed among the typeable symbols of the Sharp MZ-80K computer in 1982, in the middle of *Dr. Slump*'s run (Wikipedia: Poop emoji). One scholar attributes part of the fascination to a cross-cultural interest in the image's swirl; one critic calls the link to literal feces "tenuous" (Wikipedia: Poop emoji).

The third link is myth. The headline "Dragon Ball creator Akira Toriyama invented the emoji" exists on aggregator sites, and it has no primary evidence behind it (FandomWire). No statement by Toriyama about any emoji exists in any source. No SoftBank designer has been identified, let alone quoted on a model. No Apple designer has said the 2008 glyph came from Arale's toy. The chain from Unchi-kun to U+1F4A9 has, at its crucial 1997 link, a blank where a name should be.

### The sixth law

And yet the one person on record explaining to Google's headquarters why the glyph had to ship pointed at *Dr. Slump*. That is the sixth law of the compression instinct, and it is the one that the myth gets backward. The durable asset is not the artifact. It is the idiom. Call it idiom equity.

Toriyama did not draw the poop emoji. What he did, between 1980 and 1984, was establish a visual idiom, the smiling soft-serve swirl with a face, so thoroughly in Japanese popular culture that a decade later an anonymous designer at a phone company could draw it on a twelve-by-twelve grid and have it read instantly, and a decade after that a Google product manager could cite the comic to win an argument in Mountain View. The 1997 glyph and the golden charms and the Apple rendering all share the same grammar: swirl, smile. That is cultural authorship of a visual language. It is a real and significant thing. It is not the same as designing a glyph, and the compression instinct says it is worth more.

Jobs's position is the mirror image. He did not design a single emoji, and no source records a word from him about them. But the idiom of the single round button, the city-named font, the box that sets the tone, is credited to him on every copy anyone has ever made. Products that establish an idiom get credited for every implementation of it. Products that merely implement one get forgotten the moment the implementation is replaced. The anonymous designer of the 1997 swirl is the proof: the artifact survived, the name did not, and the idiom belonged to someone else all along.

### The phone that carried it

On November 21, 2008, Apple shipped iPhone OS 2.2, which contained the company's first emoji font and keyboard. The keyboard was built to work with SoftBank, Apple's partner for the iPhone's launch in Japan, on SoftBank's encoding, and it was gated to Japan; the update shipped worldwide, but the keyboard appeared only there, in one phrasing "initially for SoftBank SIM holders" (Emojipedia, "Apple Emoji Turns 10"; Wikipedia: Emoji). That partnership is the whole reason the modern Apple poop, and through Apple the modern Unicode poop, descends from a 1997 handset rather than from the DoCoMo set that never had one. Outside Japan, users unlocked the keyboard through Easter eggs in third-party apps that Apple did not endorse, the best known being Josh Gare's "Emoji" app of February 2010 (Emojipedia; Wikipedia: Emoji).

The standardization ran in parallel. Google's Mark Davis, Kat Momoi, and Markus Scherer had been petitioning the Unicode Technical Committee; in January 2009 the formal proposal, co-authored with Apple's Yasuo Kida and Peter Edberg, asked for 625 new characters; in October 2010, Unicode 6.0 standardized 722 emoji, PILE OF POO among them (Wikipedia: Emoji; Wikipedia: Poop emoji). Mac OS X Lion brought emoji to the Mac on July 20, 2011; iOS 5, later that year, let anyone in the world enable the keyboard from Settings; in 2012 Apple moved to Unicode code points, the year Kurita dates the overseas takeoff to; in 2015 the keyboard was on by default. Apple's set grew from 471 glyphs in 2008 to 2,776 in 2018 (Emojipedia, "Apple Emoji Turns 10"; CNN Style).

Jobs was chief executive through August 24, 2011, when he wrote, "I hereby resign as CEO of Apple," and recommended Tim Cook (Apple Newsroom). Every decision above through the development of iOS 5 was made on his watch. And that is all that can be said. No source records a statement by Jobs about emoji, or any account of his personal involvement. The "Jobs era" framing is chronological, not evidentiary. The three-year gating of the keyboard to Japan, from November 2008 to iOS 5 in 2011, coincides with the Unicode standardization of October 2010, which is consistent with Apple waiting for interoperability before a global rollout, but no source states Apple's reasoning. What is fair to say is this: the iPhone, which Jobs had announced on January 9, 2007, as "literally five years ahead of any other mobile phone," became the vehicle through which a Japanese manga-derived alphabet reached the planet, and Apple's rendering, the grinning poop, the glossy faces, set the visual standard that other vendors converged on. The product carried the idiom. The idiom predated the product.

Jobs died in Palo Alto on October 5, 2011, at fifty-six, twelve years to the day after he had stood on a stage and eulogized Akio Morita of Sony (Britannica; Nippon.com). iOS 5, released later that year, opened the emoji keyboard to everyone. The last great product of the man who had renamed the fonts after world-class cities became, in the year of his death, the global distribution channel for a character set drawn on a twelve-pixel grid by a Japanese telephone company. He left no comment. He did not need to. The pipe had done the work.

### The seventh law: slow shapes

Here is what the emoji story proves about time, and it is the seventh and last law. A shape designed for the narrowest pipe does not date. A shape designed for a wide pipe dates the moment the pipe changes. Call the first kind slow shapes: shapes with a long half-life, because they were born with nothing in them that could decay.

The evidence runs across every lineage in this story. The red heart went from a 1995 pager to a 1999 phone to Unicode without a redesign. Chicago went from a 1984 Macintosh to early iPods. The 1997 swirl-and-smile survives in every vendor's rendering of the glyph to this day; a 2017 proposal to Unicode for a FROWNING PILE OF POO failed after typographers objected, which means the one thing you cannot do to the glyph is change its face (Wikipedia: Poop emoji; Unicode L2/17-407). In 2021 the glyph ranked 98th of 1,549 emoji by frequency of use, up from 227th two years earlier, and in 2022 a survey found it the least-liked emoji in the United States (Wikipedia: Poop emoji). Among the most used and the least liked at once: that is a glyph doing exactly what Unchi-kun did, carrying a joke whose meaning the sender controls.

Now the counter-evidence, which proves the same law from the other side. Apple's emoji were drawn for a 2008 phone, and they were pictures: shaded, glossy, lit. In 2016 the company's vice president of user-interface design, Alan Dye, explained a redesign: "if we wanted to display our emojis at a larger scale, we really had to rerender them" (Emojipedia, "Apple Emoji Turns 10"). An icon designed for 144 pixels is a character. An illustration designed for a high-resolution screen must be redrawn every time the screen changes. Kurita, watching his set grow from 176 to the more than 3,700 that MoMA's wall label counted in 2025, drew the same distinction with less diplomacy:

> "Contemporary emoji aren't really emoji… the majority of them are simply pictures, I think." … "Because it makes inputting them difficult, there might also now be too many."
> — Shigetaka Kurita (CNN Style; MoMA)

A pictogram that needs eighteen bytes is a character. A rendered illustration is a picture. The constraint was the point. The 2015 Oxford Word of the Year was a face with tears of joy, the first pictograph ever chosen, in a year when use of the word "emoji" more than tripled (InfoDocket). The 2016 MoMA acquisition put Kurita's grid of glyphs into the collection of its Department of Architecture and Design. Both honors went to the compressed originals, not to the pictures that replaced them. The half-life of a shape is set at birth, by the width of the pipe it was born in, and the shapes born in the narrowest pipes are the ones museums keep.

## Part IX: The Next Hundred Years (2024– )

### March 2024

Toriyama died on March 1, 2024, at sixty-eight, of an acute subdural hematoma. His studio announced it a week later, on March 8, and said the funeral had already been held with his family and very few relatives, as he had wished; no flowers, no gifts, no visits (Gematsu; PBS/AP). What happened next is the best available measurement of how far a compressed shape can travel.

Japan's Chief Cabinet Secretary said the government was deeply saddened, and credited the work with raising Japan's international profile and bringing tourists to the country. China's Foreign Ministry spokeswoman expressed condolences. The President of France posted a drawing Toriyama had signed for him. Jackie Chan, whose *Drunken Master* had started it all, thanked him on Weibo for "creating so many classic works." Three of Europe's largest football clubs paid their respects. And in Buenos Aires, tens of thousands of people gathered at the obelisk in the center of the city and performed, together, the Genki Dama, the signature gesture of the hero of *Dragon Ball* (NBC News; Tokyo Weekender). No one had to explain the gesture. No one had to translate it. It is a silhouette.

Eiichiro Oda, who draws *One Piece*, said: "He showed us the dream that manga can go worldwide. It was like watching a hero going forward," and, "His existence is like a big tree." Masashi Kishimoto said the work had been "salvation" for "a rural boy" like him (Euronews; GMA News). Toriyama's last published contribution appeared on March 28, 2024: a redrawn page of a *Dragon Ball Super* chapter (Wikipedia: Akira Toriyama). That October, a television series he had been told would be made without him, and on which, according to its executive producer, he had ended up "crafting the entire story," premiered in Tokyo with his name on the story and the character designs (Wikipedia: Dragon Ball Daima).

Two men born forty days apart. One died in 2011, the other in 2024. Between them they left a procedure, seven laws, and one small object where their lineages crossed. The question that remains is what the procedure does next, and here the register has to change, from what happened to what might, with the change marked plainly whenever it occurs.

### The pipe narrows again

For thirty years the pipes got wider. Memory went from 128 kilobytes to gigabytes. Screens went from one bit to millions of colors. Emoji went from eighteen bytes to full-color illustrations. On that trajectory the compression instinct looks like a relic, a discipline that mattered when bandwidth was scarce and matters less every year.

If the pattern holds, the opposite is about to happen, and the reason is that the scarce resource has changed. Bytes are no longer the narrow pipe. Attention is. A wristwatch is read in a glance of perhaps a second, on a screen the size of a postage stamp, with the arm half raised. Apple's post-Jobs system typeface, San Francisco, was introduced with a watch in 2015 and only then became the face of the Mac and the iPhone (Wikipedia: Typography of Apple Inc.): the smallest screen set the standard for the largest, which is the worst-screen rule applied by a company that had been taught it in 1984. Glasses, if they arrive as a mass medium, will be read in the periphery of a person's vision while that person is doing something else. An in-car display is read at highway speed, in daylight, by a driver who must not look at it for more than a moment. Every one of these is a 144-pixel grid in a different costume. The constraint is not storage. It is a human being's glance.

This is speculation, and here is the speculative claim. The next decade of interface design will be decided by the same question Horii's puddle posed to Toriyama in 1986: what is the fewest marks that still carry the identity? The watch face, the glanceable notification, the single icon in the corner of a windshield, are the Slime problem again. The designers who solve it will be the ones who design for the floor first. The designers who fail will be the ones who draw the hero image and "scale it down."

### Machines that draw their own interfaces

A second change is harder to see and, if the pattern holds, larger. Software is beginning to generate its own interfaces on the fly, assembling screens, controls, and explanations from a description of what the person in front of it needs, rather than from a fixed layout a designer drew in advance. Speculate for a moment about what that does to the two alphabets.

A generated interface cannot rely on a learned vocabulary unless that vocabulary is standardized, because there is no designer in the loop to make sure the user has seen this control before. So the learned layer will have to be small, conventional, and shared, the way a sweat drop was shared by every reader of *Jump* in 1999 and the way 722 glyphs were shared by every phone after October 2010. And a generated interface cannot rely on a novel shape, because novelty is exactly what a person cannot parse in a glance. So the pre-linguistic layer will have to carry the identity: contour, gesture, face. The compression instinct, which looked like a craft discipline for people with pens, turns out to be the specification for a protocol between humans and machines that draw.

Emoji are the first draft of that protocol. That is the boldest claim here, and it should be read as an argument, not a finding. Before 2010, the pictographic set that Japanese phones used had three incompatible encodings and almost no life outside Japan. After October 2010, it had one encoding and, within a few years, every vendor on Earth. Unicode did for the manga alphabet what the alphabet did for speech: it turned a local vocabulary into a transmissible one. Oxford's 2015 decision to name a pictograph its Word of the Year was a lexicographer conceding that a character drawn on a Japanese phone grid was now a word in English (InfoDocket). By 2025 the set had grown past 3,700 characters, and Kurita was warning that most of them were "simply pictures" and that there might "now be too many" (MoMA; CNN Style).

Take his warning as the design brief for the next thirty years. A protocol needs a small, stable, slow core: the heart, the face, the drop, the swirl, shapes that have not needed a redesign in a quarter of a century. It needs a learned layer that is standardized and finite, like Cohn's seventy-three schemas, not an endless catalogue of illustrations. And it needs an empty face, because a machine that wants a person to feel addressed should transmit as little face as possible and let the person supply the rest. If the pattern holds, the pictographic layer between people and software will be built on exactly the glyphs that were drawn under the harshest constraints, and the glyphs that were drawn as pictures will be the ones nobody uses.

### Who holds the veto

There is a third change, and it concerns the numbered no. When interfaces are generated rather than drawn, who rejects the five hundred pages?

Torishima's doctrine is, read today, a surprisingly complete answer. The survey holds the veto: usage data, the thing readers actually do, kills what is not working, and no editor-in-chief may overrule it. But the survey reflects only one moment, so a human forms a hypothesis "half step into the future" and decides what to try next (Wikipedia: Kazuhiko Torishima). Veto by data, direction by judgment. A generating system that optimizes only against the survey will produce whatever got clicked last week, which is how a magazine fills with imitations of its last hit. A generating system with no survey at all will produce whatever its designers found pleasing, which is how Toriyama's *Wonder Island* finished last. The structure that produced *Dragon Ball* had both, in a fixed relationship, with a human in the editor's chair and a number on the desk. If the pattern holds, that is the structure that will produce interfaces people love without being told to.

Markkula's three words survive the transition untouched. Empathy: understand what the person needs. Focus: eliminate distractions, which a generating system will produce in unlimited quantity unless something says no. Impute: a machine that draws a sloppy screen has shipped a sloppy product, whatever the code underneath. The compression instinct does not care who is doing the drawing.

### A designer with two rulers, in 2050

Now the thought experiment this story has been building toward. Imagine a designer, born about now, who comes to interfaces the way Jobs and Toriyama came to theirs: from next door. Say she spent two years with an edged pen, learning why an italic hand resembles printed type, why the space between two letters is a design decision, why "a letter is a letter, not a picture of anything." And say she spent her evenings watching Hong Kong kung-fu films dozens of times, learning that a fight is choreography, that the camera cuts the walk to the airport, that each sequence must escalate or the audience feels the level has gone down. Put her in front of a heads-up display in 2050, a surface that floats in a person's field of view while they walk down a street. What does she do?

This is speculation from start to finish, but the laws predict it. She designs for the floor: the narrowest condition, a person glancing sideways for a third of a second in sunlight. She removes until it breaks, then puts one mark back. She refuses the veneer; the display shows nothing that is not a signal, because a glyph that is only decoration is noise in a channel whose whole capacity is a glance. She keeps the learned layer tiny and conventional, so that nothing on the display has to be taught. She puts the identity in a silhouette, a gesture, a face with the fewest possible marks, so that the person wearing the display fills the gap with themselves. She paces the interface like a fight: nothing arrives before it is needed, nothing lingers after, and when something matters it escalates visibly, the way blank hair glows on a black page. And she submits every decision to a number she cannot argue with and a hypothesis half a step ahead of it.

None of that requires technology that does not exist. It requires a monk's ruler, a flyer's ruler, an editor's no, and a grid. If the pattern holds, the person who builds the first interface that disappears into a glance will not be a specialist in interfaces. She will be someone who learned, in a discipline next door, what a stranger notices first.

### The physics of a civilization's marks

Step back far enough and the whole story is about one thing. A civilization is a system for transmitting meaning between people who will never meet, across distances and years, through channels that are always narrower than the meaning. Every writing system is a compression scheme. Reynolds's calligraphy course began its history of the alphabet with a Hebrew lesson written with a reed pen, because the alphabet is where the compression started: marks that had once been pictures stopped being pictures of anything (Reed College, "The Calligraphy Heritage at Reed"). What this story documents is that process running again, in living memory, in the opposite direction: pictures compressed into characters, characters standardized into a script, the script adopted by a planet in about a decade.

If the pattern holds, that will not be the last time. Every new channel between humans and machines, every narrower glance and smaller surface and faster exchange, will demand its own compression, and the shapes that win will have the same four properties as the Slime and the city font and the eighteen-byte heart. The physics does not change. The pipe always narrows somewhere. And the instinct that finds the signal in the narrowest pipe is the one that gets to decide what the future looks like, one glyph at a time.

## Part X: A Field Manual for Compression

What follows is the procedure, stated as law, with the single piece of evidence that earns each law and the single instruction that follows from it. It is short because the subject demands it.

**1. Borrow the ruler from next door.** Calligraphy gave Jobs the eye for letter-pair spacing and advertising gave Toriyama his intolerance for an uneven line; neither standard came from the field it was applied to. Find the adjacent discipline whose quality bar your own field has stopped being able to see, learn it until it hurts, and bring the bar home.

**2. Install a numbered no.** Torishima's survey, Markkula's three words, and Kōbun's demand for proof were all judgments the creator could not overrule by argument, and some of the roughly five hundred rejected pages were reused as stories later, not wasted. Put someone outside the work in charge of saying no, give them an instrument with a number on it, and let a hypothesis half a step ahead of the number decide what to try next.

**3. Design for the worst screen first.** The Slime was built to be identifiable "even when they're small," Chicago was built with no diagonals for a one-bit screen, and Kurita's glyphs were built for 144 pixels; none has needed a redesign. Start from the smallest, dimmest, fastest condition the thing will ever face, make the identity survive there, and only then let it grow.

**4. Treat the constraint as the specification.** Eighteen bytes produced emoji, no money for screentone produced Toriyama's solid blacks, and 128 kilobytes produced the Macintosh. When you find yourself asking for more memory, time, or people before you can make something good, suspect that the design is not yet reduced to its signal.

**5. Run the convenience gradient.** Toriyama randomized the rocks and escalated the fights; Jobs removed the fan and lavished the box. Spend nothing where the user is not looking, and spend everything you saved where they are.

**6. Leave the face empty.** Two dots and a curve let every player of *Dragon Quest* and every sender of the poop emoji put themselves inside the shape, which is why the shapes cross cultures without translation. Transmit as little identity as the object needs and let the receiver supply the rest.

**7. Keep the learned alphabet small.** Goku's story was *Journey to the West*, Geneva was a redesigned Helvetica, and Kurita's set was manga shorthand everyone in Japan already knew; the originality went into the silhouette. Use conventions the audience already has, and spend novelty only on the shape that carries the identity.

**8. Impute everything the user touches, including names.** "World class cities" was an evening's decision that Apple still honors with San Francisco and New York, and Horii named his monsters only after seeing Toriyama's drawings. Give the name, the first screen, and the box the rigor you give the circuit board, because the user reads them first.

**9. Own the idiom, not the artifact.** Toriyama drew no emoji and is credited with the poop; the anonymous designer of the 1997 glyph drew it and is credited with nothing. Build the visual language others will have to reimplement, and let the implementations belong to whoever wants them.

**10. Prefer slow shapes.** The red heart went from a 1995 pager to Unicode unchanged, while Apple's 2008 illustrations had to be rerendered in 2016 to survive a bigger screen. Choose the version of the mark that has nothing in it that can date, and then do not touch it when the cast changes.

**11. Let the work be the proof.** Kōbun would not accept a claim of enlightenment without a concrete example, and Toriyama refused any title grander than "a gag-manga artist after all." Make no claim for the design that the object itself does not make when it is sixteen pixels tall and the person looking at it has never heard of you.

## Coda: The Passer-By

Go back to Nagoya. It is 1977, or near enough. A young man who gets home at three in the morning is late again, and the firm's rule says three lates make an absence, and his pay in his third year is lower than it should be, and the client only sells hens. He has done the arithmetic. He is going to quit. He is going to ask his mother for money. He is going to send a parody of a space movie to a contest he is not eligible for, and a stranger is going to read it and see nothing but the lettering, and the lettering is going to be enough.

He will not much like the work that replaces it, either, and he will say so in 2003 and 2010 and 2013. He will draw fourteen pages a week for over a decade, often on almost no sleep, and call himself lazy. He will give a monster a face so that a child can kill it without feeling bad and feel bad anyway. He will make his hero's hair white because inking it black takes too long, and a generation will learn from that blank space what power looks like. He will draw, on the covers of a comic about a robot girl, a German flying wing from a model kit, and inside the pages a smiling swirl of excrement with feet, and he will never say a word about where the swirl went after it left his page.

Across the ocean, a boy born forty days before him has already sat, uninvited, in a calligraphy class taught by a monk who will never use a computer, learning the width of the space between two letters, and is now selling a computer whose brochure says that simplicity is the ultimate sophistication. In six years he will walk into a room at night and look at a font menu and say the names are not good enough. In thirty-one years his company will ship a keyboard, built for a Japanese partner, that carries the swirl into every pocket on Earth, and he will not mention it either.

A flyer has no reader. It has a passer-by. The young man in Nagoya hated that, and learned it, and drew for the passer-by for the rest of his life. The passer-by turned out to be everyone.

## Notes and sources

Quotations and dates in the text are drawn from the sources below, cited inline by short name. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is stated in the text: Reed College's main page credits Lloyd Reynolds with the calligraphy class, while Reed Magazine's obituaries of Jobs (2011) and Palladino (2016) and the corrected Smithsonian tribute name Robert Palladino; Toriyama's and Torishima's accounts of the roughly five hundred rejected pages agree on the number and differ on the period; Toriyama quit the design firm by his own account and no primary source says he was dismissed; J-Phone's November 1, 1997 set precedes Kurita's 1998–99 set, as Kurita acknowledged in January 2019; no source records any statement or decision by Jobs concerning emoji; and no primary source links the 1997 poop glyph to *Dr. Slump* beyond the cultural priming described by Google's Takeshi Kishimoto in 2014.
